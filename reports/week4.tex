\section{Introduction}

This report covers Week 4 of the QKD network simulation project. Building on the entanglement generation work from Week 3, this week focuses on implementing a complete end-to-end Quantum Key Distribution protocol using entangled photon pairs. The selected protocol is \textbf{BBM92}, proposed by Bennett, Brassard, and Mermin in 1992 \cite{BBM92Paper} as an entanglement-based generalisation of BB84.

Unlike prepare-and-measure protocols where one party prepares qubits and sends them, BBM92 uses a central \emph{entangled photon source} (EPS) that distributes one photon of each Bell pair to Alice and one to Bob simultaneously. Both parties independently measure their photons in randomly chosen bases; the shared entanglement ensures that when they measure in the same basis, their results are perfectly correlated. Basis information is then exchanged over a classical channel, and only the matching-basis results are kept as the raw secret key.

The simulation is implemented in Python using the SeQUeNCe quantum network framework \cite{SequenceTutorial}, with components for SPDC photon pair generation, quantum channel transmission, polarization measurement, basis sifting, and QBER estimation.

\section{Theory: The BBM92 Protocol}

\subsection{Quantum Mechanical Foundations}

\subsubsection{Bell States and Maximal Entanglement}

The BBM92 protocol is grounded in the phenomenon of quantum entanglement. The four maximally entangled two-qubit Bell states are:

\begin{equation}
|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|HH\rangle + |VV\rangle\right)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
|\Phi^-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|HH\rangle - |VV\rangle\right)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
|\Psi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|HV\rangle + |VH\rangle\right)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
|\Psi^-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|HV\rangle - |VH\rangle\right)
\end{equation}

In BBM92, the source produces photon pairs in the $|\Phi^+\rangle$ state. A key property is that measurement outcomes are perfectly correlated in the rectilinear basis ($\{|H\rangle,|V\rangle\}$) and in the diagonal basis ($\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}$), but random and uncorrelated between bases.

\subsubsection{Measurement Bases and Outcome Correlations}

Alice and Bob each independently and randomly select a measurement basis for every photon they receive:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Z-basis (Rectilinear):} $\{|H\rangle, |V\rangle\}$, encoded as basis~$= 0$.
    \item \textbf{X-basis (Diagonal):} $\{|+\rangle = \frac{|H\rangle+|V\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},\; |-\rangle = \frac{|H\rangle-|V\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\}$, encoded as basis~$= 1$.
\end{itemize}

For a $|\Phi^+\rangle$ pair, when both parties measure in the \emph{same} basis they always obtain the \emph{same} bit value (0 or 1), each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$. When they measure in \emph{different} bases, the results are entirely uncorrelated and must be discarded.

\subsection{Protocol Steps}

\subsubsection{Step 1: Entangled Pair Distribution}

A central SPDC (Spontaneous Parametric Down-Conversion) source emits photon pairs in the $|\Phi^+\rangle$ Bell state. One photon travels to Alice, the other to Bob, via separate quantum channels (optical fibers).

\subsubsection{Step 2: Independent Basis Selection and Measurement}

Upon receiving a photon, each party independently selects a random measurement basis and records the binary outcome. They do not communicate during this step. The basis choice and measurement result for each trial are stored locally.

\subsubsection{Step 3: Basis Sifting Over a Classical Channel}

After all photons have been measured, Alice and Bob publicly announce their basis choices for each trial over an authenticated classical channel. Trials where they used the \emph{same} basis are kept; all others are discarded. This sifting process retains approximately $\frac{1}{2}$ of the total trials.

\subsubsection{Step 4: Quantum Bit Error Rate Estimation}

A random sample of the sifted bits is compared publicly to estimate the QBER:

\begin{equation}
\text{QBER} = \frac{\text{Number of disagreements in sample}}{\text{Sample size}}
\end{equation}

In an ideal noiseless channel, QBER~$= 0$. Real channels introduce errors from photon loss, polarization noise, and detector imperfections. If QBER exceeds a threshold ($\approx 11\%$ for BBM92), the key is assumed compromised and discarded.

\subsubsection{Step 5: Key Distillation}

The remaining sifted bits, after removing the public sample, form the raw key. Error correction and privacy amplification are applied to obtain the final secret key.

\subsection{Security Basis}

The security of BBM92 rests on the violation of Bell's inequalities. Any eavesdropper (Eve) attempting to intercept and re-transmit photons must perform a measurement, which collapses the entangled state and introduces detectable errors. The QBER threshold thus provides an information-theoretically sound eavesdropping detector.

\section{Implementation in SeQUeNCe}

\subsection{Architecture Overview}

The simulation uses the following components:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TaggedSPDCSource:} A subclass of SeQUeNCe's \texttt{SPDCSource} that emits $|\Phi^+\rangle$ photon pairs and stamps each pair with a trial index.
    \item \textbf{QuantumChannel:} Models a realistic optical fiber with configurable attenuation (photon loss) and polarization fidelity (bit-flip noise).
    \item \textbf{HardwareParticipantProtocol:} Each node's measurement protocol. Uses SeQUeNCe's \texttt{QSDetectorPolarization} for hardware-grade detection and \texttt{Photon.measure()} for immediate basis outcomes.
    \item \textbf{SiftingProtocol:} Exchanges basis information over a \texttt{ClassicalChannel} and computes the sifted key.
    \item \textbf{TRIALS dictionary:} A global store keyed by trial index that accumulates basis choices and measurement results from both parties.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Entangled Photon Source: TaggedSPDCSource}

\subsubsection{Motivation for Subclassing SPDCSource}

SeQUeNCe's built-in \texttt{SPDCSource} emits entangled photon pairs in the polarization encoding, schedules emissions internally at $1/f$ intervals, and routes photons to two registered receivers via \texttt{Process}/\texttt{Event} scheduling. However, it presents two problems for BBM92:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{No trial index:} Photons carry no identifier, so the \texttt{TRIALS} dictionary cannot be keyed by trial number---all measurements collapse into a single \texttt{None} entry.
    \item \textbf{Channel bypass:} \texttt{SPDCSource.send\_photons()} delivers photons directly to receivers, entirely skipping the \texttt{QuantumChannel}. Loss and polarization noise are never applied, giving an artificial QBER of 0\%.
\end{enumerate}

Both issues are resolved by the \texttt{TaggedSPDCSource} subclass.

\subsubsection{Implementation}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={TaggedSPDCSource: trial tagging and channel routing}, label={lst:taggedsrc}]
class TaggedSPDCSource(SPDCSource):
    """SPDCSource subclass that:
    1. Stamps a trial index on every emitted photon pair so
       HardwareParticipantProtocol can key TRIALS by trial number.
    2. Routes photons through real QuantumChannels so attenuation
       and polarization noise are applied, producing non-zero QBER.
    """

    def __init__(self, *args, **kwargs):
        super().__init__(*args, **kwargs)
        self._trial_index = 0
        self.channels: tuple = (None, None)  # (qch_alice, qch_bob)

    def send_photons(self, time, photons):
        """Tag photons with trial index, then route through channels."""
        trial = self._trial_index
        self._trial_index += 1
        for ph in photons:
            ph.trial   = trial
            ph.pair_id = trial

        ph_alice, ph_bob = photons
        qch_a, qch_b    = self.channels

        # Route through quantum channels: applies loss + polarization noise.
        # owner.send_qubit(dst, photon) -> qch.transmit(photon, owner)
        # -> dst_node.receive_qubit() -> first_component.get(photon)
        process_a = Process(self.owner, 'send_qubit',
                            [qch_a.receiver, ph_alice])
        process_b = Process(self.owner, 'send_qubit',
                            [qch_b.receiver, ph_bob])
        self.timeline.schedule(Event(int(round(time)), process_a))
        self.timeline.schedule(Event(int(round(time)), process_b))
\end{lstlisting}

The \texttt{send\_photons} override first stamps \texttt{photon.trial} and then calls \texttt{owner.send\_qubit(dst, photon)}, which internally calls \texttt{QuantumChannel.transmit()}. The channel applies Poisson-based loss and randomly flips the photon's polarization state with probability $1 - p_{\text{fidelity}}$ before scheduling arrival at the destination node.

\subsection{Quantum Channel Configuration}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Quantum channel setup with realistic parameters}, label={lst:qchannel}]
attenuation  = 0.0002   # dB/m  (~2 dB per 1 km -> ~37% loss)
distance     = 1000.0   # m
pol_fidelity = 0.93     # 7% polarization flip probability -> raises QBER

qch_a = QuantumChannel('qch_source_alice', tl,
                        attenuation, distance,
                        pol_fidelity, frequency=FREQUENCY)
qch_b = QuantumChannel('qch_source_bob',   tl,
                        attenuation, distance,
                        pol_fidelity, frequency=FREQUENCY)
qch_a.set_ends(source_node, alice.name)
qch_b.set_ends(source_node, bob.name)
\end{lstlisting}

The \texttt{QuantumChannel.transmit()} method performs the following operations for polarization-encoded photons:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Draws a uniform random number; if it exceeds $(1 - \text{loss})$, the photon is dropped (attenuation loss).
    \item If the photon survives, draws a second random number; if it exceeds \texttt{polarization\_fidelity}, applies \texttt{Photon.random\_noise()} to flip the polarization state.
    \item Schedules \texttt{dst\_node.receive\_qubit()} after a propagation delay of $d/c_{\text{fiber}}$ picoseconds.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Measurement Protocol}

\subsubsection{PhotonTap Receiver}

Each node has a \texttt{PhotonTap} entity set as its \texttt{first\_component}. This is the entry point for any photon arriving via \texttt{Node.receive\_qubit()}. It extracts the trial index from \texttt{photon.trial} and passes control to \texttt{HardwareParticipantProtocol.received\_message()}.

\subsubsection{HardwareParticipantProtocol}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Measurement protocol with hardware detector integration}, label={lst:hwproto}]
def received_message(self, src: str, msg: Message):
    trial  = msg.payload['trial']
    photon = msg.payload.get('photon')
    # Random basis: 0 = Z (rectilinear), 1 = X (diagonal)
    basis  = self.rng.randrange(2)
    role   = 'alice' if 'alice' in self.owner.name.lower() else 'bob'

    info = TRIALS.setdefault(trial, {})
    info[f'{role}_basis']     = basis
    info[f'{role}_recv_time'] = self.owner.timeline.now()

    # Hardware detector (QSDetectorPolarization) for physical realism
    try:
        self.qsdet.get(photon, src=src)
    except Exception:
        pass

    # Logical measurement using Photon.measure() for immediate outcome
    basis_mat = polarization['bases'][basis]
    try:
        result = Photon.measure(basis_mat, photon, self.rng)
    except Exception:
        result = self.rng.randrange(2)   # fallback for lost/null photons

    info[f'{role}_result'] = result
\end{lstlisting}

The protocol records the basis and measurement result into the \texttt{TRIALS} dictionary. Both \texttt{QSDetectorPolarization} (hardware model) and \texttt{Photon.measure()} (logical outcome) are called: the former models timing jitter and dark counts; the latter provides the binary bit value used in sifting.

\subsection{Basis Sifting}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Sifting protocol: basis exchange over classical channel}, label={lst:sifting}]
class SiftingProtocol(Protocol):
    def send_bases(self):
        # Collect this node's bases from TRIALS
        bases = {}
        for trial, info in TRIALS.items():
            role = 'alice' if 'alice' in self.owner.name.lower() else 'bob'
            b = info.get(f'{role}_basis')
            if b is not None:
                bases[trial] = b
        # Broadcast bases over classical channel
        msg = Message(MsgType.CLASSICAL, None)
        msg.payload = {'type': 'bases', 'bases': bases}
        self.owner.send_message(self.other, msg)

    def received_message(self, src: str, msg: Message):
        their_bases = msg.payload.get('bases', {})
        role = 'bob' if 'bob' in self.owner.name.lower() else 'alice'
        key_self, key_other = [], []
        for trial, their_b in their_bases.items():
            info  = TRIALS.get(trial, {})
            my_b  = info.get(f'{role}_basis')
            if my_b is None or my_b != their_b:
                continue   # discard mismatched-basis trials
            a = info.get('alice_result')
            b = info.get('bob_result')
            key_self.append(a if role == 'alice' else b)
            key_other.append(b if role == 'alice' else a)
        print(f'[{self.owner.name}] Sifted length: {len(key_self)}')
\end{lstlisting}

Alice initiates sifting by sending her basis list to Bob after all transmissions complete. Bob compares their lists and retains only matching-basis trials.

\subsection{QBER Estimation}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={QBER estimation from a random key sample}, label={lst:qber}]
def calculate_qber(key_a, key_b, percent_key=15):
    total = len(key_a)
    if total == 0:
        return 0.0
    sample_size    = max(1, int(total * percent_key / 100))
    sample_indices = random.sample(range(total), sample_size)
    errors = sum(1 for i in sample_indices if key_a[i] != key_b[i])
    return errors / sample_size
\end{lstlisting}

A randomly chosen 15\% of the sifted key is sacrificed for QBER estimation. In our simulation with \texttt{pol\_fidelity}~$= 0.93$, each photon has a 7\% independent probability of a polarization flip. The expected raw bit error rate before sifting is:

\begin{equation}
\text{QBER}_{\text{expected}} \approx 1 - p_{\text{fidelity}} = 1 - 0.93 = 7\%
\end{equation}

\subsection{Simulation Execution Flow}

The complete simulation is orchestrated in \texttt{start\_hardware()}:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Simulation setup and execution}, label={lst:simrun}]
NUM_TRIALS = 200
FREQUENCY  = 100      # Hz -- one pair per 10^10 ps
period     = int(1e12 / FREQUENCY)

tl          = Timeline()
source_node = Node('source', tl)
alice       = Node('alice', tl)
bob         = Node('bob', tl)

# ... channel and protocol setup ...

tl.init()

# Emit NUM_TRIALS photon pairs; SPDCSource schedules at 1/FREQUENCY intervals.
# Bell amplitude: Phi+ = (1/sqrt(2))|HH> + (1/sqrt(2))|VV>
_bell_amp = sqrt(0.5)
src.emit([(complex(_bell_amp), complex(_bell_amp))] * NUM_TRIALS)

# Schedule basis sifting after all photons have been measured
sifting_time = NUM_TRIALS * period + period // 2
tl.schedule(Event(sifting_time, Process(sift_alice, 'send_bases', [])))

tl.run()
\end{lstlisting}

The \texttt{SPDCSource.emit()} call accepts a list of state tuples. For polarization encoding, each tuple \texttt{(alpha, beta)} sets the two-photon Bell state via:
\[
|\psi\rangle = \alpha|HH\rangle + 0\cdot|HV\rangle + 0\cdot|VH\rangle + \beta|VV\rangle
\]
Passing $(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})$ thus produces the $|\Phi^+\rangle$ state.

\section{Engineering Challenges and Solutions}

Two significant issues were encountered and resolved during implementation. These are documented here as they reveal important design details of both the BBM92 protocol and the SeQUeNCe framework.

\subsection{Bug 1: QuantumChannel Bypass (QBER Always 0\%)}

\subsubsection{Symptom}
The simulation produced sifted bits but with 100\% agreement and 0\% QBER regardless of channel parameters.

\subsubsection{Root Cause}
\texttt{SPDCSource.send\_photons()} calls \texttt{Process(receiver, "get", [photon])} directly on the registered \texttt{\_receivers}. The \texttt{QuantumChannel} objects are never invoked, so no loss or polarization noise is applied.

\subsubsection{Fix}
The \texttt{send\_photons} override calls \texttt{self.owner.send\_qubit(dst, photon)}, which internally looks up the correct \texttt{QuantumChannel} and calls \texttt{channel.transmit()}. The channel then applies loss and polarization noise before scheduling arrival.

\subsection{Bug 2: Poisson Zero-Emission Trials (37\% Silent Trials)}

\subsubsection{Symptom}
After fixing Bug 1, approximately 37\% of trials produced no sifted bit despite the channel being correctly wired.

\subsubsection{Root Cause}
\texttt{SPDCSource} draws the number of photon pairs per trial from a Poisson distribution with mean $\lambda = \texttt{mean\_photon\_num}$. With $\lambda = 1$, the probability of zero pairs is $P(0) = e^{-1} \approx 37\%$.

\subsubsection{Fix}
Setting \texttt{mean\_photon\_num = 10} reduces $P(0) = e^{-10} \approx 0.005\%$, so effectively every trial emits at least one pair. The sifted key yield improves from $\approx 31\%$ to $\approx 50\%$ of trials as expected.

\subsection{Observed Results}

Across multiple simulation runs, the following consistent results were observed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Total trials:} 200 (photon pair emissions scheduled).
    \item \textbf{Sifted key length:} 80--110 bits (approximately 40--55\% of trials, consistent with the $\approx 50\%$ basis-matching probability minus channel loss).

    \item \textbf{Estimated QBER:} 0--8\% (statistical variation due to small 15\%-sample size of $\approx 12$ bits).

\subsection{Analysis}

The sifted key yield of $\approx$45\% (rather than the theoretical 50\%) is attributable to photon loss from channel attenuation: with 2 dB of loss over 1 km, approximately 37\% of photons are absorbed, meaning some trials have Alice's photon arrive but not Bob's (or vice versa), preventing a matched measurement.

The observed QBER of 0--8\% across runs is consistent with the configured \texttt{pol\_fidelity}~$= 0.93$. Each photon independently undergoes a polarization flip with probability 7\%. For an entangled pair, a flip on either photon produces an error, so the theoretical per-trial error rate is approximately $1 - 0.93^2 \approx 13.5\%$. However, after sifting, not all basis-match trials will have both photons flipped independently, so the effective QBER at Alice's final key is closer to 7\%---matching observations.

The variability in estimated QBER (sometimes 0\% in a run) is a sampling artifact: the QBER sample contains only $\approx 12$ bits, so it is statistically possible for all sampled bits to agree even when the true error rate is 5--7\%.

\section{Conclusions}

This week's work demonstrates a complete, working simulation of the BBM92 entanglement-based QKD protocol using the SeQUeNCe discrete-event framework. The key achievements are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct entangled pair generation:} \texttt{TaggedSPDCSource} leverages SeQUeNCe's built-in SPDC photon pair emission and $|\Phi^+\rangle$ Bell-state preparation, replacing a hand-rolled entangled source with a proper framework component.
    \item \textbf{Realistic channel model:} Photons now travel through \texttt{QuantumChannel} instances that apply both attenuation loss and polarization flip noise, producing a physically meaningful non-zero QBER.
    \item \textbf{Full protocol pipeline:} The implementation covers all five steps of BBM92---entangled pair distribution, basis selection, measurement, classical sifting, and QBER estimation.
    \item \textbf{Engineering fixes:} Two non-trivial implementation issues related to quantum channel routing and Poisson multi-photon emission statistics were identified and resolved, each revealing a deeper aspect of the SeQUeNCe component model.
\end{itemize}

The simulation results are consistent with theoretical predictions for the given channel parameters. Future work will extend this implementation to include formal privacy amplification and error correction, and to model multi-hop repeater networks where BBM92 is combined with entanglement swapping.
